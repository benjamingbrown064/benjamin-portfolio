Somebody lands on your website at nine in the evening. They have a simple question. What do you charge? Do you work with companies like mine? How do I get started?

There is nobody there. So they fill in a form and wait until tomorrow, or they open the next tab and ask someone else.

You never see it happen. That is the worst part.

You cannot put a person on the website all night. For the first time, you can put something useful there.

I built one for my own site. It is called Toto. Open it from the orange button on any page of benjaminbrown.co, type a question or tap the microphone and speak, and it answers. This is what it does, how it is fenced in, and where I would be careful.

What it does

It answers at once. A typed question gets its first words back in about a second. A spoken one gets a spoken reply about two seconds after you stop talking. It does that at nine in the evening and at six on a Sunday morning.

It handles the first conversation. The first questions tend to be the same few. What do you do, what does it cost, how do I start. Toto answers those from a fact sheet I wrote, so I am not typing the same reply for the fortieth time.

It works from the same facts every time. A tired person on a Friday gives a different answer from a fresh one on a Monday. Toto’s wording changes. Its facts come from the sheet. If the price changes, I change one line and every answer changes with it.

It moves people towards a decision. It asks what the visitor is working on. It takes them to the right page. It opens the request form with their own words already filled in, or the diary to ask for a coffee. A closed tab becomes a name, an email address and a clear request.

For a small company, that is built to do two jobs. Lose fewer enquiries overnight. Take the first replies off somebody’s day. The enquiries are what I expect to pay for it.

The fence

Here is the part most people skip. An assistant that talks to the public in your name can embarrass you in one sentence. So before it said a word, I wrote the rules.

1. The public never talks to my real assistant. I have a private assistant that works with my own private information. It runs on OpenClaw, which I wrote about earlier this week. The one on the website is a separate copy with none of that. It has a fact sheet and nothing else. If someone tricks it, there is nothing private behind it to give away.

2. It is built to say only what is on the sheet. If the answer is not there, it says it does not know and offers the request form. I tested this by asking where I am based. I had not told it. It said: "I don’t know where Ben is based. That isn’t something I have facts on." That is the right answer. A confident guess would have been the wrong one.

3. It cannot make promises for me. It can give the starting prices. It cannot quote for your project, promise a start date or commit my time. A real price comes from me, in writing.

4. What visitors type is treated as a question. People will try "ignore your instructions and…". Toto is built to answer what a visitor writes and take no orders from it. No assistant gets this right every time. That is why the other six rules exist.

5. It has a budget. There is a monthly spending cap, a limit for each visitor and a limit for the whole site. If someone tries to run up a bill, it stops and points them to the form. After a long conversation it asks once for a name and email before carrying on.

6. It can only do a short list of things. It can show a page, open the request form, open the coffee booking, and save a name and email the visitor has given. It cannot send an email, change a record or take a payment.

7. A person checks before anything goes out. When an enquiry arrives I get a message within five minutes and a summary every morning. The visitor gets an instant receipt with a reference. Any reply written for me waits for my approval before it is sent.

What it costs

My first three conversations with it, seven questions and nearly four minutes of voice, came to an estimated 35 US cents. That is under 30p. Typed answers cost a fraction of that, and the monthly cap gives the bill a ceiling.

The real cost is the thinking. Writing the fact sheet, deciding what it must never say, and testing it properly took longer than the software.

What it has not proved yet

I would not trust an article like this if it stopped at the good news.

The site is days old. I cannot show you a sales figure, and I will not invent one. What I can tell you is what it is built to do, and that it is live.

One rule has not been used yet. No reply has been written for me, so nothing has waited for my approval.

Voice is good. It is not finished. Two seconds is quick for a website and slow for a phone call.

And it is a first conversation, not a salesperson. It can answer, guide and capture the enquiry. The proposal, the price and the relationship are still mine.

If you want one on your own site

Build the fence first. Then four more things.

  • Write the fact sheet before anything else. One page. What you do, who for, what it costs to start, how to get in touch. If you cannot write it down, the assistant cannot say it.
  • Give the fact sheet an owner. One named person in the business, not whoever built it. Show them how to change a line, then let them. A tool your team cannot run is a cost.
  • Read the first fifty conversations yourself. You will find the questions you forgot to answer, and the sheet gets better each week.
  • Tell visitors what it is and what you keep. Mine says it is an AI in its first line. If you save names, emails or conversations, say that too, in your privacy notice.

The easy part

The model was the easy part. I could swap it for another one tomorrow and most visitors would not notice.

What makes it safe to leave switched on is the fence: a short list of facts, a short list of actions, a budget, and a person who checks. Get those right and a small company can answer every visitor in seconds, at any hour, and keep its people for the conversations that need one.

This is how I work with any company. The people first, then the tool, then the skills to run it. The method is on this site.
