Someone lands on your website at nine at night. They’ve got a simple question. What do you charge? Do you work with companies like mine? How do I start?

Nobody’s there. So they fill in a form and wait till tomorrow. Or they open the next tab and ask someone else.

You never see it happen. That’s the bit that gets me.

You can’t put a person on the website all night. For the first time, you can put something useful there.

So I built one for my own site. It’s called Toto. Click the orange button on any page, type a question or tap the mic and talk, and it answers. Here’s what it does, how it’s fenced in, and where I’d be careful.

What it does

It answers straight away.

Type a question and the first words come back in about a second. Ask out loud and it talks back about two seconds after you stop. At nine at night. At six on a Sunday morning.

It handles the first conversation.

The first questions are nearly always the same few. What do you do, what does it cost, how do I start. Toto answers from a fact sheet I wrote, so I’m not typing the same reply for the fortieth time.

It works from the same facts every time.

I give a different answer on a tired Friday than on a fresh Monday. Everyone does. Toto’s wording changes, but its facts come from the sheet. If a price changes, I change one line and every answer changes with it.

It moves people along.

It asks what they’re working on. It takes them to the right page. It opens the request form with their own words already in it, or the diary to book a coffee. A closed tab turns into a name, an email and a clear request.

For a small company, that’s two jobs. Lose fewer enquiries overnight. Take the first replies off someone’s plate.

The enquiries are what I expect to pay for it. Expect, not know. More on that below.

The fence

This is the part most people skip. An assistant that talks to the public in your name can embarrass you in one sentence. So before it said a word, I wrote the rules.

1. The public never talks to my real assistant.

I’ve got a private assistant that works with my own stuff. It runs on OpenClaw, which I wrote about yesterday. The one on the website is a separate copy with none of that. It’s got a fact sheet and nothing else. If someone tricks it, there’s nothing private behind it to give away.

2. It’s built to say only what’s on the sheet.

If the answer isn’t there, it says it doesn’t know and offers the request form. I asked it whether I’d ever worked with a charity. That isn’t on the sheet. It said: “I don’t know. Nothing I have mentions Ben working with a charity.” Then it offered to put the question to me. That’s the right answer. A confident guess would have been the wrong one.

3. It can’t make promises for me.

It can give the starting prices. It can’t quote for your project, promise a start date or commit my time. A real price comes from me, in writing.

4. Whatever visitors type is treated as a question.

People will try “ignore your instructions and…”. Toto’s built to answer what someone writes and take no orders from it. No assistant gets that right every time. Which is exactly why the other six rules exist.

5. It has a budget.

There’s a monthly cap, a limit per visitor and a limit for the whole site. If someone tries to run up a bill, it stops and points them to the form. After a long chat, it asks once for a name and email before carrying on.

6. It can only do a few things.

Show a page. Open the request form. Open the coffee booking. Save a name and email someone’s given it. It can’t send an email, change a record or take a payment.

7. A person checks before anything goes out.

When an enquiry comes in, I get a message within five minutes and a summary every morning. The visitor gets an instant receipt with a reference. Any reply written for me waits for me to approve it.

What it costs

My first three conversations with it, seven questions and nearly four minutes of voice, came to an estimated 35 US cents. Under 30p. Typed answers cost a fraction of that, and the monthly cap puts a ceiling on the bill.

The real cost is the thinking.

Writing the fact sheet, deciding what it must never say, and testing it properly took longer than building it.

What it hasn’t proved yet

I wouldn’t trust an article like this if it stopped at the good news.

And it’s a first conversation, not a salesperson. It can answer, guide and catch the enquiry. The proposal, the price and the relationship are still mine.

If you want one on your own site

Build the fence first. Then four more things.

The easy part

The model was the easy part, honestly. I could swap it for another one tomorrow and most people wouldn’t notice.

What makes it safe to leave switched on is the fence. A short list of facts, a short list of actions, a budget, and a person who checks.

Get those right and a small company can answer everyone in seconds, at any hour, and keep its people for the conversations that need a person.

It’s how I work with any company. People first, then the tool, then the skills to run it. The method’s on this site.

Click the orange button and ask it something. Then ask it something it shouldn’t answer, and see what it does.
