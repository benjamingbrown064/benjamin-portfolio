Someone in your next meeting might not be a person.

As of this week, an AI can join the call, answer questions in the chat, write the notes and send the actions round afterwards. That’s one of five things that changed in four days.

Most weeks in AI are noise. This one wasn’t. The tools got closer to the work you already do. The good ones got cheaper. And one company found out what an unlocked AI account can cost. $740,000.

Here are the five that matter if you run a business, and one thing to do about each.

1. It sits in your meetings now

On 11 November, Grok Bot 1.0 started joining Google Meet, Zoom and Microsoft Teams calls. It answers in the meeting chat and sends a summary afterwards. It’s on Team, Enterprise and Ultra plans.

A day later, Claude Meetings went into beta on Team and Enterprise plans. Google Meet only, for now. Claude joins as a visible participant, writes the notes and actions, and can start a document or a board while you talk.

So nobody has to type up the notes. Read them the way you’d read a new starter’s first set of minutes, though. It might miss the tone, get a name wrong, or turn a loose idea into a firm action.

This weekTry it on one internal meeting. Not a client call. Read the action list before it goes anywhere, and fix what isn’t right.

2. Claude Fable 5.2 got faster, and 20% cheaper

Anthropic released Claude Fable 5.2 on 9 November. It’s up to 30% faster than Fable 5.1 and 20% cheaper on the API, at $8 to send and $40 to receive per million tokens. It’s built for long, multi-step work.

If you already pay for Claude, the new model’s in the app. You don’t need to think about tokens or API prices to use it.

This weekFind the long piece of work you keep putting off. Open it on Fable 5.2. Give it a clear brief and one example of what good looks like.

3. People are asking ChatGPT who to call

People already ask ChatGPT for a recommendation before they open Google. This week you got a say in what it finds.

On 10 November, OpenAI launched Businesses in ChatGPT. Any eligible business in a country where ChatGPT works can claim its profile, keep its details up to date, and see how often ChatGPT mentions it and sends clicks its way. In the US, some online shops and service businesses can also build a Business Agent, a branded assistant customers can chat with.

It’s the nearest thing yet to a Google Business Profile for AI search. If your listing’s empty or out of date, you’re leaving that conversation to chance.

This weekClaim your business. See which topics already mention you. Fix anything that’s wrong or missing.

4. Don’t put your best person on the filing

You wouldn’t ask your most senior person to do the filing. Same goes for AI models.

Google spent the week cutting prices. On 10 November it released Gemini 4 Carbon, its strongest model, at an introductory $2 to send and $10 to receive per million tokens until 30 November, then $4 and $20. On 12 November it followed with Gemini 4 Hydrogen, a faster, cheaper option at $1.50 and $7.50.

A token’s just the unit these services use to measure how much text goes in and out. The price is what you pay per million of them.

Use the strong model for hard, long work. Use a cheaper, quicker one for drafting, sorting and first goes.

This weekPick one routine job you already run through ChatGPT, Claude or Grok Bot. Try it on a cheaper or faster model. Keep the strong one for the work that needs it.

5. One stolen key. A $740,000 bill.

An AI key is a password that can spend money in your name.

On 10 November, Railway published a report on a security attack in October. The attackers went after customers’ AI keys. Using those stolen keys ran up an estimated $740,000.

You don’t need to be a Railway customer for this to matter. If a key leaks, the bill can move faster than anyone notices.

This weekTreat every AI key like a company card. Set a spending limit where you can. Change any key that’s been sitting around for months.

  • Claude launched Sheets, Whiteboards and Data on all paid plans, so plans and simple spreadsheets can stay in Claude.

One to watch

One to watch, though it didn’t ship this week: OpenClaw Enterprise, launched on 29 September. It’s a free tool a company can run on its own servers to keep AI assistants under its own security and permissions. Still pre-1.0, so pilots only.

The new starter

Put the five together and AI starts to look a lot like a new starter. It’s got a seat in the meeting, a listing people can find, a day rate and, if you’re not careful, a company card.

You wouldn’t hand a new starter the card on day one and leave the building. A meeting summary still needs a person to check it. A cheaper model still needs a clear brief.

Give it a job. Look at what comes back. Only then let it do more.
