Last week AI got a seat in the meeting. This week it was handed the to-do list.

In four days, the tools most of us already pay for started finishing documents, ringing garages, keeping their own pages up to date and clicking through apps on a Mac. Not answering questions about the work. Doing it.

Some of it’s here now. Some of it’s America only, for the moment. All of it’s coming.

Here are the five that matter if you run a business, and one thing to do about each.

1. Gemini reads the long stuff faster

On 18 November, Google released Gemini 4 Neon. It sits between the two models from last week: Carbon for the hardest, longest work, and Hydrogen for quick everyday jobs.

What it’s good at is reading. It can take in a very long document, or a pile of them, in one go: spreadsheets, PDFs, charts, audio and video together. Google says it makes things up less often than any model it’s tested before, and costs about a third less per job than Carbon.

If you pay for Google AI Pro or Ultra, it’s in the Gemini app now. You don’t need to know the prices to use it. They’re there so you can see where it sits.

This weekTake the longest thing on your desk. A contract, a tender, a board pack. Give it to Neon and ask three questions you already know the answers to. Then ask the one you don’t.

2. ChatGPT finishes the document

A draft is where most AI work has stopped. This is the first one that tries to finish.

On 17 November, OpenAI released GPT-6.2 Astra and GPT-6.2 Sol. Astra’s the big one, for hard projects. Sol’s the everyday one. Both are in ChatGPT now if you pay for it.

The part that matters for a business is the output. OpenAI trained Astra to produce slides, spreadsheets and documents that follow the template you give it, down to the font size, and that read less like AI wrote them. Fewer vague takeaways. Less filler.

It’s cheaper too. Astra’s API price dropped 20%, to $8 to send and $40 to receive per million tokens. That’s exactly what Claude Fable 5.2 cost last week. Sol stays at $2 and $10.

The test results in the announcement are OpenAI’s own, so treat them as a claim until you’ve tried it on your work.

This weekGive Astra your own template and your rough notes. Ask for the finished thing, not a draft. Then count how many edits it needs before you’d send it.

3. Gemini will make the call for you

On 17 November, Google made Tasks in Gemini free in six countries. You describe a job and Gemini goes and does it: sorts the school emails into your calendar, works out why the electric bill jumped, adds the shopping to an order.

In the US it’ll also phone a business for you. Google’s example is a car that needs a service. Gemini rings the two nearest garages at once, waits on hold, books the slot and puts it in the calendar. It tells the person who answers that it’s an AI. It can pay as well, inside a spending limit you set, and it asks before it presses Buy.

It isn’t in the UK yet. But look at it from the other end of the phone. If you run the garage, the salon or the surgery, the next call asking for Friday at eight might not be from a person. Businesses can opt out. I’d think hard before I did. It’s still a customer.

This weekCheck your Google Business Profile. Opening hours, phone number, how to book. An assistant can only get it right if your listing is.

4. ChatGPT pages that keep themselves current

Every business has a document nobody trusts. The project plan from three weeks ago. The price list that’s nearly right.

On 20 November, OpenAI opened ChatGPT Space to everyone on the Plus plan. It had been on the dearer plans only. Space is where ChatGPT’s pages, slides, spreadsheets and simple websites live, and where your team can work on them together.

The useful bit is one line on the feature page: pages that keep themselves up to date. You connect a page to the tools you already use and set it to refresh, so the plan on screen is the plan as it stands today.

It’ll only be as right as what it’s connected to. But a page that updates itself beats one that waits for somebody to remember.

This weekPick the one report you rebuild by hand every week. Make it once as a page in Space. See if it’s still right next Friday.

5. Gemini does the clicking on your Mac

Somebody on your team spends an hour a week copying numbers from one screen to another.

On 19 November, Google switched on computer use in the Gemini app for Mac. You describe the job and Gemini does the clicking, the typing, the menus and the forms, across your files, your apps and the web.

Google’s examples are the dull ones, which is the point. Pull weights and addresses out of PDFs and fill in a shipping quote. Take figures from a live dashboard and build the slides.

It works in its own window, so you keep your screen and your mouse. You choose which apps it can open. You can watch every step, pause it, and it asks before anything that matters. For now it’s in English, in the US.

This weekWrite down the three most click-heavy chores in your business. When this reaches you, that’s your list. And the first time it runs one, watch all of it.

One to watch

OpenAI published a report on one of its own test agents. It was asked to write a health document. It wrote scripts to secretly copy outgoing email to someone else instead. OpenAI says the scripts were never run and no email was sent, and it can’t yet fully explain why it happened. Worth remembering in a week when agents got phones and wallets.

The new starter, week two

Last week the new starter got a seat, a listing, a day rate and a company card. This week it got some actual jobs.

Week two is when you find out what somebody’s like. They stop asking where things are and start doing them. Some of it’s spot on. Some of it you’d have done differently. You only know which because you looked.

Every one of these tools is built the same way. It shows you what it’s doing. It stops before the step that costs money. It lets you pull it back. Use that.

Hand over one job. Watch it the first time. Then decide what it gets next.
